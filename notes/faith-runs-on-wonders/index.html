--- title: "Faith Runs on Wonders" date: 2026-11-13 tags: [Personal, Religion] ---

“Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and the more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above and the moral law within.”

— Immanuel Kant, Critique of Practical Reason (trans. T. K. Abbott)

Belief, perchance, is the most subjective concept. What might appear as an absolute truth to one person might be a mere conjecture to another. Take the planetary model, for example: when Aristarchus of Samos1 presented the heliocentric model in the 3rd century BCE, his idea was dismissed partly because it did not align with the prevailing beliefs at that time. It was not until the mid-16th century that a few astronomers (such as Copernicus2) started to take heliocentrism seriously. However, one must always remember that belief is not the same as knowledge, for we are liable to err.

I know of no subject on which belief outruns knowledge as far as it does in religion, where claims win credence with an alacrity3 seldom shown elsewhere. Religion, as I understand it, is a collection of tenets: a rulebook, ordained by a deity, on how to spend one’s life, and much of our morality, ethics, and law has long been grounded in revelation. When one troubles oneself with the deepest questions, such as the origins of the universe, life, consciousness, and free will, one eventually arrives at the ineluctable4 debate around the existence of God. I ask a more specific question: what persuades a proud animal to believe in a god?

I have been asked this question in the past, and since then I have asked a few more persons: what is your chief reason to believe? One friend replied, untroubled by doubt, that he beholds the world, perceives design in it, and infers from it a creator. The answers vary, but most are familiar responses which readers will recognize. I shall list some such arguments: Cosmological, Contingency, Ontological, Teleological (Design), Argument from Desire, Argument from Testimony, and so on. From what I have heard and read, the argument from divine justice might be one of the most prevalent reasons. A despondent person has a propensity to find solace in the hope that justice awaits in a life to come. This notion is alluring if all one experienced so far is suffering.

I shall not debate here, nor do I intend to lambaste any belief system. I will say only this: no belief earns the right to call itself the only objective one. We must zoom out, observe from various perspectives, and weigh the possibilities. I aspire to take this approach in every aspect of life, and this question was no exception. Once I looked at the bigger picture, both in contemporary times and in historical records, one pattern stood out. Insofar as I know, all these arguments try to justify the existence of a deity. Yet, I surmise, without one key ingredient, most of them would be quite precarious, and faith itself would be rarer and more contested. That ingredient is miracles. By a miracle I mean an event so amazing that people credit it to a higher power.

A man who never met the Supreme at work, whether first-hand or vicariously, would have little motive to believe in a higher power. This might raise some suspicions of the sort: People once believed the Sun and the Moon to be the Creators, yet no miracle transpired in those moments; the Sun never spoke, nor did the Moon ever pour gold. This objection is specious, for it is oblivious to context. If one must indulge in these historical examples, one should consider events in the light of that era. People took the Sun and Moon for deities because they marveled at them, and because a harvest, a rain, or an eclipse seemed theirs to grant or withhold. Given what they knew, their conclusion is understandable.

In other words, such events appeared miraculous. With a little hindsight, it becomes clear that what counts as extraordinary shifts with the zeitgeist5, and no one can say how amazing is amazing enough.

The scriptures of so many faiths6 abound in miraculous occurrences, and this is no accident. The salvation of Abraham from fire7, the parting of the Red Sea, or the resurrection of Jesus are manifestations of Divine Love for humankind, and the adulations follow. The Flood, Sodom, the plagues of Egypt8, and the fate of ‘Ad and Thamud9 teach that the hand which saves may also strike, and a God who can strike is not easily forgotten. Lest we suffer his wrath, we are told to assuage his anger with a mountain of apologies. Rescue or ruin, the believer reads each as a display of omnipotence, hard to dismiss, and is apt to be left in awe and wonder.

A fair objection stands here, and I should not hurry past it: a skeptic might notice that perchance I have mistaken the cause for the effect. Most believers keep the traditions of their ancestors and never weigh a miracle at all. I am no expert, and I am far from infallible. Most of us do inherit our faith as we inherit our mother tongue. Yet every inheritance has a first giver. Somebody believed before there was anyone to copy, and I ask what moved him. The tradition answers for itself: he saw, or was said to have seen, a wonder. Lest the wonder be forgotten, he took care to inculcate it in his progeny.

But is there a faith that requires no wonders? Kant thought so. He called the faith of tradition historical10: it rests on miracles, revelation, and the word of witnesses, and serves as a vehicle for a purer faith of conscience, which needs no wonder at all. I hold no quarrel with him. However, a ladder is still a ladder, and very few can reach the upper rung without climbing the lower. Such a believer of conscience, I suspect, was once a child who heard a miraculous story.

If faith rests on wonders, then for most believers it rests on testimony, a precarious ground. Fantastical claims require commensurate11 evidence, and miracles, being the biggest wonder of all, must exhibit it in the fullest measure. I, for one, am not moved by vicarious wonders. A friend recently asked me what it would take for me to believe again. I answered at once (after a bit of contemplation, of course): a miracle of my own, experienced first-hand. Were the wonder to come, would I believe my own eyes?

Annotations

  1. Aristarchus of Samos: Greek astronomer (c. 310–c. 230 BCE) who proposed that the Earth orbits the Sun. A moving Earth should have shown stellar parallax, which no one could observe. ↩
  2. Copernicus: Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) published his Sun-centred model in 1543. ↩
  3. Alacrity: brisk, cheerful readiness. ↩
  4. Ineluctable: impossible to avoid or escape. ↩
  5. Zeitgeist: German for "spirit of the age": the ideas and mood typical of an era. ↩
  6. So many faiths: my examples come from Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, because I know them best. ↩
  7. Abraham from fire: told in the Quran (21:68–70) and in Jewish legend (Ginzberg, Legends of the Jews), not in Genesis. ↩
  8. The Flood, Sodom, the plagues: Genesis 6–9 and 18–19; Exodus 7–12. ↩
  9. ‘Ad and Thamud: Arabian peoples that the Quran says were destroyed after rejecting their prophets Hud and Salih. ↩
  10. Historical faith: Kant's term (also "ecclesiastical faith") in Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason (1793) for actual religious traditions. He saw them as a vehicle for a pure moral faith. ↩
  11. Commensurate: in proportion to something else; of matching size or degree. ↩
