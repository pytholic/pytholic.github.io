--- title: "Concerning the Concealed Half of Existence" date: 2026-11-06 tags: [Personal, Philosophy] ---

“The bird fights its way out of the egg. The egg is the world. Who would be born must first destroy a world. The bird flies to God. That God's name is Abraxas.”

— Hermann Hesse, Demian

Quite often I find myself contemplating this quote. The idea of a deity that holds both halves, the good and the bad, suggests that we, as humans, should acknowledge all our intrinsic dispositions, instead of being coerced to subdue certain proclivities. The question always confounds me: why are we so adamant about abjuring turpitude while acknowledging all our rectitude? I do not judge what is “right” and “wrong”, nor do I consider myself sagacious enough for that. The accepted standards are a vexed question, whether you are practicing asceticism or hold a different tenet of morality. Not that one may pick an axe and behead anyone he likes. But I speak of the innate desires — some of which are quite innocuous.

I hold that a savage nature dwells in us, inherited and shaped through evolution. It is no defect that crept in, nor one to be extirpated. Sex may have spared our line deleterious mutations (Muller’s Ratchet), yet some traditions condemn it, and where shame forbids the subject, ignorance takes its toll in disease and unwanted births. Hunting fed our ancestors, yet many now call it cruelty. My point is that, if we still carry out such innate acts, we would do better to acknowledge them than to wallow in denial and dissonance. Questioning faith fares worse: insofar as I know, apostasy is punishable by death in some traditions and some states. In company we wear a mask of virtue; in the dark we keep the instincts it forbids. Pride, anger, envy, vanity: to recapitulate, we are not obliged to follow them, but we ought not to pretend they are absent, nor be compelled to do penance for having them. We are not made cleaner by forgetting what made us.

It takes little introspection and observation to see that the bright parts of human nature are kindred to the dark. Kindness, loyalty, kinship, empathy are likely a product of natural selection over millions of years. Vice and virtue differ in their effects, but we have no sufficient evidence that they differ in their origin. Indeed, the evidence corroborates a common source: the faculty to read another’s pain serves the healer and the torturer alike. Why then do we attribute all the light to God, whilst encumbering the Devil with our vices?

Akin to evil is the problem of suffering; where one is, the other is seldom far. Yet suffering needs no vice to bring it: the world holds pain that no one has caused. Pleasure and pain come from the same machinery. Nature has no volition. It is blind. Strictly, even my premise that nature holds both halves is loose, for nature is indifferent; good and evil are merely names we give. We humans, with our fallible minds, tend to anthropomorphize everything. A frog might also, on occasion, fancy itself the centre of the pond. What stands is this: we cannot keep the pleasure and be rid of the pain; they are entangled.

The strongest objection is that these desires harm other people. I grant the harm; but to conceal a desire is not to remove it, and what we cannot see we cannot guard against. Lest I be misunderstood: that nature is thus does not mean that we ought to be thus; whether it is good or bad needs its own essay. Let us acquiesce in what we are and drop the dissimulation, for much of the harm lies not in the desire but in our denial of it. And if we would understand our existence and our origins, we must first reckon with what made us: nature.

Theology has long offered answers, and the matter would be incomplete without them; I intend no polemic against any faith. In Christianity, evil traces back to us, through free will or the Fall, while God remains wholly pure. In Islam, suffering exists to test humankind and raise it toward a life of faith. At their best, such tenets may abate the vices; at their worst, they lead to oppression, revolt and extremism. Even so, gratuitous suffering remains unjustified. Perchance the Gnostic Christians had it right. Some held that the world was made not by the supreme God but by a lesser maker, the Demiurge, born of the lapse of Sophia (which ironically means Wisdom). A hostile maker, determined to raise a world that is flawed and painful, while the true God remains distant and wholly good. You may say I indulge in mythology; you would miss the purport of my point. I, by no means, am a Gnostic, yet the world, so mixed, sits somewhat better with a flawed maker than with a wholly good one. Both views, however, are palliations, easing the problem without curing its cause.

“One is most dishonest towards one’s God: he is not permitted to sin!”

— Friedrich Nietzsche

Abraxas asks no such price. Presented in the early second-century teachings of Basilides, Abraxas was later made by Jung and Hesse a deity who holds the divine and the demonic alike. I take him as a symbol, nothing more. Hume held that a wise man proportions his belief to the evidence. I have not yet witnessed or experienced sufficient evidence for the existence of a God. Notwithstanding, I feel a resonance with the notion of such a god. I do not urge anyone to follow a new deity; I say only that, were one to look to a higher power, the evidence before us fits a deity that is not wholly pure.

Hesse’s bird does not leave the egg gently. It fights its way out, and the egg is the half-world that we are handed. To be born is to break that picture, and the breaking has a cost: we are no more unstained. The shell was never the whole sky.

“If you can't discover what's keeping you in, the will to get out soon becomes confused and ineffectual.”

— Daniel Quinn, Ishmael

So I return to my question. We adulate the saintly and disown the bestial, as though they lived in different bodies. To look at the whole of ourselves will trouble us far more than it comforts. Yet the bird that leaves the egg must fly high to see beyond the forest and outgrow its solipsism. From on high, it shall find no saints and no beasts below, only life, tangled and whole.
