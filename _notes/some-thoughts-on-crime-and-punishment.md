<blockquote class="epigraph">
<p>“You sensed that you should be following a different path, a more ambitious one, you felt that you were destined for other things but you had no idea how to achieve them and in your misery you began to hate everything around you.”</p>
<cite>— Fyodor Dostoevsky, <i>Netochka Nezvanova</i></cite>
</blockquote>

I recently finished *Crime and Punishment* by Dostoevsky. It took me a while — around three weeks, maybe a bit more (it drags in places, so it might not be to everyone's taste). The novel follows the misfortune and angst of Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov (Rodya to those who love him), a former student, broke and brooding in St. Petersburg — a disquieting tale of a crime humanity deems an act of moral turpitude, and the punishment that follows it. However, the punishment is twofold. One is ordained by the law, which is the least of Raskolnikov’s concerns. The real one is internal: his disgust with himself, the paranoia, the dissonance, the slow unraveling, the inner cacophony driving him to the brink of madness, and only at the very end, the first sign of redemption.

I shall not summarize the book or dive into its details, as many have already done so, and far better than I could. Instead, I shall focus on a few points that I have in mind. First, the murder of Alyona Ivanovna and her half-sister Lizaveta; second, the ending, which is in fact the beginning of Raskolnikov’s salvation.

<hr class="section-break">

Raskolnikov committed those murders on the pretext that he was an extraordinary man, like Napoleon, who has the right to step over moral boundaries, or to whom such thoughts of good and bad never even occur. At first sight, this is appalling to say the least (from my standards), and it puzzles me. Why would an educated recluse like Raskolnikov consider murder his only option? Great men do not commit such crimes for money. If Raskolnikov considered himself one of them, why kill a nobody, an old pawnbroker, and take her purse and a few trinkets? Of course, he failed to take much, and the whole thing descended into chaos. Lizaveta wasn't even meant to be there.

He answers this while talking with Sonya. It was a test. An experiment to find out whether he was a "louse" or a man with the right to transgress.  He admits he didn't really kill for the money, and he never touches what he stole. Either way, the act was reckless and imprudent.  Afterward, Raskolnikov was so disgusted with himself: he considered himself above the morality of ignorant plebs, only to find out he was nothing more than one himself.

<hr class="section-break">

The ending, though, is a clear allusion to Christianity, especially since the story of Lazarus is read aloud to Raskolnikov earlier in the book. Raskolnikov seems like a man who despises dogma, a nihilist even, yet in the epilogue he turns toward a new guiding light, Sonya, and begins a journey toward salvation. But I wonder if it is that simple. To be fair, by then he is broken: imprisoned, ill, and shunned by the other convicts, and salvation is alluring once despair takes over. Even then, he regrets only his failure, not the murder itself. My issue is less with the transformation than with how little of it we see. After hundreds of pages inside his unraveling, the rebuilding gets a few paragraphs, and Dostoevsky admits as much, calling it the start of a new story. I understand where this comes from (Dostoevsky found his own faith in a Siberian prison), but resting the resolution on a conversion we are told about rather than shown feels like an answer only the already-convinced can accept.

As a skeptic, I find the inner workings of the mind more convincing than revelation. Hume argued that our beliefs are shaped by custom and habit. Reason is the slave of the passions. If that is true, Raskolnikov's change should have been slow and visible, not handed to us in an epilogue. Then again, perhaps that is the point. Faith, as Kierkegaard put it, is a leap: it does not wait for reasons and cannot be argued into. Dostoevsky seems to have believed this himself; he once wrote that if someone proved Christ was outside the truth, he would rather remain with Christ than with the truth. Read that way, the abruptness is deliberate. But that is also exactly why it cannot persuade anyone standing outside the faith. In short, the premise deserved a journey we never get to see.

More broadly, Dostoevsky's point is that reason and rationality do not free us from the need for morality and love. Man is a rational yet social animal, and for a society to function, we need ethics. I could dive into who defines those ethics, how we ground them, or whether there is any objective truth at all, but maybe some other time.

I do agree with the main takeaway, though. Raskolnikov’s fever dream in the epilogue, a plague that leaves everyone certain that they alone are right, says it best: if everyone believed himself to be the right one (which we still do), chaos would seep into even the most mundane parts of everyday life. I lean toward the view that morality is ultimately an expression of emotion (emotivism). How we reconcile those emotions with one another may never be settled, but Raskolnikov shows what happens when one man decides he alone has the answer.

<hr class="section-break">

As with all books, I am apt to put myself in the place of such characters. What would I do under such circumstances? Having listened to Sam Harris and Robert Sapolsky, I have a certain predilection toward determinism. Hume spoke of a uniformity in human actions, the same motives producing the same conduct across ages and cultures, but we should not assume this implies that each person will act in exactly the same manner under similar circumstances. Countless factors are at play: immediate neural activity, hormone levels and physiological state, brain plasticity, genetics, upbringing, environment, the development of the prefrontal cortex, evolutionary history, and cultural norms, to name a few. It is tempting, and a little sanctimonious, to blurt out that no sane person would go on killing the feeble members of society for personal gain or as an experiment. I do agree.

However, I daresay, put yourself under such conditions: born and raised as our Rodya was, facing similar ordeals. What would you have done? What would I do? If not murder, I suspect we might still be capable of other vile acts. After all, as Schopenhauer would say, we are driven by a blind will-to-live that always wants more, be it wealth, power, or proof of our own worth.. What shall we do then?

That brings me to my personal takeaway from the book: compassion and empathy, and the deprecation of retributive blame, punishment, and shame. Dostoevsky would likely share the compassion but not the reasoning. He mocks the idea that crime is merely a product of environment, and he redeems Raskolnikov through suffering rather than sparing him from it. For him, mercy presupposes guilt. Yet even his court shows mercy, shortening the sentence because of the confession, his illness, and his past kindnesses. But mercy after the fact is only half the answer. Consider what led Rodya there: poverty that drove him out of university, weeks of hunger and fever in a cramped garret, his mother's letter announcing Dunya's sacrifice for his sake, and an idea that grew unchecked in his isolation. None of this excuses the axe, but each was a point where someone could have stepped in. So I think we should go further and revisit not only our judicial systems but also the conditions that feed them, treating harmful behavior as something to understand and prevent rather than making an example of such people. It is a daunting task that might take generations, as history shows, and I am not sure this is the one right way either, but there is only one way to find out. Science and curiosity have brought us a long way, and we won’t be wrong to expect even more revelations in the coming decades. The more we discover, the more we realize how little we know. Times change, and so shall we. As Chinua Achebe wrote:

<blockquote class="epigraph closing">
<p>“The world has no end, and what is good among one people is an abomination with others.”</p>
<cite>— Chinua Achebe, <i>Things Fall Apart</i></cite>
</blockquote>
